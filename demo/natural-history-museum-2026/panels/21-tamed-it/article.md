# I Think I Tamed It in the End

The animatronic *Tyrannosaurus* diorama, deep in the dinosaur halls. Sixty-six million years ago, this animal would have been the worst thing that could happen to your afternoon. Now, in a darkened Friday-evening gallery in South Kensington, it lurches and roars on a slow electric loop, lit theatrically, surrounded by visitors with phones up.

Charlotte, in a red dress, walked straight up to it.

There is a photograph of her doing this. She is in profile, the rex is in profile, and the gap between them is about a metre. She is small and bright; the rex is large and yellow-grey and very pleased with itself. They are eye to eye. The composition is from a stage play that hasn't been written yet, in which a woman in red negotiates terms with a six-tonne reconstruction. Whatever was said in the negotiation went well.

Then Graham joined her, and they took the second picture. Both of them grinning. The rex looming behind them, lit blue and gold, half blurred. Two people who have spent the evening pointing each other at sloths and slime moulds and emperor penguins, and have arrived, finally, at a posed selfie under a dinosaur.

Graham's caption:

> *"I think I tamed it in the end."*
> — Graham

This is the picture, I suspect, that the whole scrapbook has been building toward.

You can spend an evening in this museum looking at the largest animal that ever lived suspended in a glass cathedral, and at a slime mould fifteen millimetres tall, and at every wavelength of light, and at every scale of life and death and recovery. You can spend ninety minutes inside a wildlife photography exhibition where the *world itself* keeps recalibrating. You can read every placard. You can ask each other "how big do you think these are?" and "what would be your first choice?" and "can you tell what this is?". You can absorb a lot of detail.

But the keeper image, the one that gets framed, isn't of the whale or the fern or the bats. The keeper image is the two of you, grinning, slightly knackered, at the end of the rooms, with a tyrannosaurus over your shoulder.

The museum is, in the end, just the wallpaper.
