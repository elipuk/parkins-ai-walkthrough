# You Can Smell Them Before You See Them

> *"You can smell them before you see them."*
> — Graham

The photograph: an ibex, silhouetted on a high crag, head and curling horns turned slightly into the wind. A second animal lower down, resting on a smaller outcrop. The peak rises out of drifting cloud, and the cloud is catching golden hour — peach and apricot through the upper sky, the rock cooling into pale grey beneath. The light is doing the kind of thing light only does for fifteen minutes a day, and only in mountains.

It is, on the face of it, a religious photograph. Cathedral light. Mountain saint. The eye says: *quiet, ancient, dignified.*

Graham's caption arrives, removes its hat, and pulls the rug out.

You can smell them before you see them.

Anyone who has ever walked up a Pyrenean valley in autumn or a Scottish glen during the goat rut knows this is not a slur on the ibex. It is a *biological compliment*. Male wild goats and ibex deliberately reek. They scent-mark — onto themselves, onto rocks, onto each other — with the urine-and-glandular cocktail that, to a female ibex, is the most attractive aftershave on the mountain. To a human downwind, it is a smell that lives in your sinuses for the rest of the day. You'll be home, in the shower, two hundred miles away, and you'll think *oh that was the goats*.

The photograph cannot show you that. Photography has not yet cracked the olfactory. So the picture sells you the cathedral, and only the person who was actually there knows the cathedral smells like a billy goat's bedroom.

The exhibition is doing this all evening, panel after panel, just in different keys: showing you the *visible* slice of an animal's world and quietly admitting it's only one slice. Wavelengths we can't see. Scales we can't read. And now: smells we can't transmit. Every photograph is a sketch of a much bigger sensory whole, and the sketch is honest about being a sketch.

Graham, helpfully, supplies the missing channel.

You can smell them before you see them. Quite right.
