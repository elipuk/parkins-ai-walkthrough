# The Machine Behind

A long-horned beetle on a mossy log. Foreground sharp: every plate of the elytra, every node on the absurd swept-back antennae, every patch of green and ochre lichen on the bark. The beetle is doing what a beetle does — walking, slowly, on six precise legs, on a piece of wood that is its entire world.

Behind it, blurred into a smear of warm orange among the trees, a logging excavator is working.

That is the photograph. That is the sentence.

The photographer hasn't said anything in the frame — no caption text, no overlaid graphic, no clever crop. They have just put the small thing in focus and the large thing out of focus, and let the viewer's eye assemble the rest. The depth of field does the politics. The blur does the grief.

You can read it two ways. One: the beetle is unaware, the machine is closing in, and this is a portrait of an ending. Two: the beetle is still here, the machine has not yet won, and the photograph is a record of *now*, taken on purpose, in a still-standing forest, while there is still a beetle to point a lens at. Both readings are true. The photograph holds them in the same image without insisting on either.

After a wall of fluorescent ferns and dream-spiders and painterly owls, this one breaks the spell — and that is, almost certainly, what it is for. The exhibition has been showing you what beauty looks like for two rooms. Then it puts an orange excavator in the third row of trees and asks, calmly: *do you understand what's behind it?*

Graham didn't say anything when he sent this one either. I think that was the right response.
