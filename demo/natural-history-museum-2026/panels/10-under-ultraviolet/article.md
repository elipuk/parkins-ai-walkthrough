# Under Ultraviolet

Two more from the same wall as the [glowing fern](../08-celestial-spores/). No placards this time, so no photographers to credit by name — just the images and the strangeness.

## The Pitcher Plants

A cluster of *Nepenthes* pitchers — jug-shaped carnivorous traps, normally a sober mottled green and burgundy — lit so they glow. Pinks down the lips of the jugs, soft cyan inside the throats, the surrounding leaves and ferns turning lurid in the same wavelength. They look less like plants and more like a chemistry diagram. Which, in fairness, is partly what they are: each pitcher is a small acid bath for insects, doing the work that roots can't do in nutrient-poor soils. Under UV the trap chemistry shows up, and the plant suddenly looks like the predator it actually is. Ordinary daylight is a polite lie that these plants have been telling for millennia.

## The Spider in the Bouquet

The second image stops you. A jet-black background. Floating across it, a constellation of luminous shapes — orange flares, blue ovals, pale ghostly halos — that look at first like a dream sequence or an X-ray of something delicate. In the very middle, dead solid black, the only object in the frame that doesn't fluoresce: a small spider, legs spread, the unmoving anchor that lets you parse everything else as *real.*

The bright shapes are probably blooms or unidentifiable plant structures glowing under the same UV — the spider, having nothing in it that fluoresces in that range, simply punches a hole through the picture in the shape of a spider. The composition is almost certainly accidental. It also looks composed by a Dutch master.

The exhibition has a *thing* about ultraviolet this year — three rooms in, three images that use wavelengths none of us can see directly. The argument seems to be: the world you can see is a subset; the world your camera can see with the right filter is bigger; the world the wasps and the spiders and the birds see is bigger still. And there is, apparently, a beauty argument hidden in the chemistry that nobody knows about because nobody normally points a UV torch at a fern.

Three photographers, three different subjects, the same hidden colours. A small genre quietly being invented in front of us.
