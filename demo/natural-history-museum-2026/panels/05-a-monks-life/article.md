# A Monk's Life

*Photograph by Greg Lecoeur. Highly Commended, Wildlife Photographer of the Year. Photographed at the entrance to a sea-cave in Greece; Nikon D500 with a Tokina 10–17mm lens.*

A Mediterranean monk seal, hanging just under the surface at the mouth of a sea-cave. Whiskers fanned, mottled grey-and-cream coat catching the green of the water, two dark eyes looking calmly straight at the camera. There is nothing performative about her. She is just there, breathing, weighing the photographer up.

Lecoeur took the shot in Greece — one of the last places in Europe where you can still meet a monk seal at all. There were once thousands of them strung along the Mediterranean coast, hauling out on sandy beaches and resting in colonies. Then humans arrived: fishermen who saw competition, tourists who wanted the beaches, divers who got too close. By the 1980s they were down to a few hundred animals, scattered, persecuted, pushed into the sea-caves they now use as nurseries because the open beaches are no longer safe.

The good news — the reason this is a *Monk's* life, not a memorial — is that the numbers are climbing. Protected reserves, hands-off marine parks, and patient, unglamorous conservation work have lifted the population back to perhaps a thousand animals. Still critically endangered, still living in caves, but no longer falling.

The first thing inside the Wildlife Photographer of the Year exhibition. It rhymes, deliberately or not, with [Hope](../03-hope/) hanging in the entrance hall a few minutes' walk back: another species we very nearly finished off, still here.
