# A Leap of Faith

> *"A leap of faith. It's [a] 15m jump down."*
> — Graham

A long line of emperor penguin chicks, lined up like a queue at a bus stop, along the very edge of a tall Antarctic ice shelf. Cracked sea ice below them, dark water below that. A soft pink-grey horizon. Far-off icebergs. The chicks themselves: small, fluffy at the head, slate-feathered at the body, the unmistakable emperor markings just starting to show. They are looking down.

Fifteen metres is the height of a four-storey building.

These birds have never swum. Not once. They were born on the ice in midwinter, raised on regurgitated krill, kept warm under their parents' brood pouches through the worst weather on the planet. By the time they reach this cliff edge they are old enough to be on their own — but they have lived their entire lives in air. The water below them is something they have never tested. It is something they only *theoretically* know they can do.

They jump anyway.

That is the thing the photograph holds. Not the leap itself, but the *moment before* — the bit where you can see them weighing it. There is a small physics calculation happening behind every set of eyes: if I stay, I starve; if I go, I might do the only thing my body has ever been built for; the only way to find out is to do it. The wind picks up. One of them edges forward. The rest follow. Twenty bodies fall through fifteen metres of air and hit the sea and — every single time anybody has filmed this — *they swim*. They surface. They look around. They start swimming as if they always knew, because in a sense they always did. The instinct was there before the experience. It had just been waiting for a cliff.

There is a famous version of this image filmed by drone in the last couple of years; this isn't necessarily that one, and without a placard I'm not going to put a name on it. The behaviour, though, is the same behaviour. It's one of the most-shared frames in nature filmmaking of the last decade for a reason.

Graham's caption is the right caption. *A leap of faith.* The bird knows nothing yet, and trusts everything. There is something to be said for that.
