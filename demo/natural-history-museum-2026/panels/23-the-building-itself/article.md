# The Building Itself

The last photograph of the night. The main entrance arches of the museum, looking up: the great banded terracotta arches of pale buff and slate blue, the carved capitals, the Romanesque rhythm of column and arch and column. The evening has cooled. The crowd is thinning. Graham took the picture on the way back out.

The Waterhouse building is, secretly, the last exhibit of the visit.

Most people don't notice. They walk in through these arches focused on the whale, walk out focused on dinner, and don't look at the bricks. But every inch of the terracotta is *also* a natural history collection. Climb up the capitals with your eyes and you find: monkeys clinging to the columns, lizards along the cornices, pterodactyls in the spandrels, fish, ferns, eagles, beetles. Alfred Waterhouse and his sculptor Dujardin laid it on with deliberate intent — a sermon in clay, baked at high temperature, slotted into the skin of the building.

There is even a rule to the arrangement that the museum doesn't make a fuss about but is genuinely there. The west wing is decorated with *living* species. The east wing is decorated with *extinct* species. The plants and animals on the building are sorted by whether they were still on the planet when the bricks were laid in the 1870s, and which side of the central axis you are standing on tells you which. You can walk the perimeter and trace the boundary in terracotta. Most people never know.

It is — if you let it land — a cathedral built for nature instead of saints. Same impulse. Same scale. Same use of the surface as instruction. Where a medieval cathedral covers its piers with prophets and gospel scenes, this one covers its piers with the actual contents of the world, arranged taxonomically, fired in clay, painted in subtle pale colours so as not to interrupt the architecture.

Inside, this evening, the museum had a blue whale called Hope, a glowing fern in ultraviolet, a sloth on a fence post, a slime mould posed for a family portrait, and a Charlotte squaring up to a T. rex. *And the entire outside of the building had been holding the same conversation, in clay, since before any of them existed.*

The arches go up into the dusk. Graham and Charlotte walk out under them.

Lovely day.
