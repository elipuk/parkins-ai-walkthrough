# Morning Constitutional

> *"Hello, morning constitutional."*
> — Graham

A bird in pure silhouette. Wings flung out to either side, head tilted slightly, the whole shape rendered in black against a dawn — or dusk, you can't tell, and it doesn't matter — sky of pale pink and grey. Behind: the soft suggestion of pines, blurred by mist. A low sun, diffused into a pale disc, hangs just above the treeline like a stage light somebody forgot to turn off.

No placard for this one, so no name to put on the photographer and no species ID to put on the bird. Doesn't need either.

What's interesting is that the picture works *because* you can't see any detail. There is no plumage. There are no markings. There is no eye. There is only the shape: a body, two wings, the implication of a beak. Your brain — which has been looking at birds for longer than it has been *your* brain — does the rest. It tells you this is a bird in the act of greeting something. It might be drying its feathers after a wet night. It might be stretching. It might be displaying. It might just be enjoying the morning, in the way that creatures with wings sometimes are.

Graham's caption gives it the right reading. *Morning constitutional.* Not the Edwardian gentleman's walk-around-the-square-after-breakfast, but the bird version — same instinct, different surface area. Out into the air. Survey the territory. Lift the wings up, let the cool mist hit the undersides, see who else is about. Then get on with the day.

There's a thing photographers learn early about silhouettes, which is that subtraction is cheaper than addition. Take away the detail and you keep only the gesture. Keep only the gesture and you give the viewer the whole bird, weighted with whatever they happen to bring with them. A photo with every feather is a photo of feathers; a photo of a shape is a photo of a *moment.*

This is a moment. *Hello, morning constitutional.* Quite right.
