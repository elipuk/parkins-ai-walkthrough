# Wrong Turn

A three-toed sloth, clinging to a wooden fence post. Long arms wrapped around the timber, hook-like claws gripping the corners, that resigned permanent-half-smile face turned slightly toward the camera. Beside her, in the same frame, a strand of barbed wire — taut, rusted, deeply not designed for sloths. Behind: a flat green field, a dirt path, a bright tropical blue sky.

On the first read, this is funny. A sloth, of all animals, on a fence post. The slowest mammal on the continent has taken an inexplicable detour, like a tourist with a faulty map, and found itself improbably *out in the open*. You can almost hear it apologising.

The placard isn't here, so I can't name the photographer or give it its title. But I can read the picture, and the picture is, on the second read, a quieter, sadder kind of thing.

Sloths don't live on fence posts. Sloths live in canopies. They are tree animals, top to bottom — physiology, diet, predator avoidance, social life. They are *built* to be twenty metres up in a network of interconnecting branches, moving from leaf to leaf without ever having to come down. The whole reason a sloth is slow is that the canopy is continuous; you can take your time when your floor is also your ceiling.

This sloth is not in the canopy. This sloth is on a fence post next to a field, because the field is where the canopy used to be. The bright blue sky is too much sky. The dirt path is too much ground. The barbed wire is — for a tree animal trying to bridge a gap between two surviving patches of forest — a hazard of a category nothing in her ancestry has prepared her for. She is doing the only thing she can do, which is the slow shuffle from one tree to another, across what should be more trees and is now agriculture.

It's a habitat-fragmentation photograph dressed up as a comedy. That's what makes it work. A picture of a clear-felled rainforest with the captions removed would scroll past; a picture of a sloth looking faintly bewildered on a fence post stops you, makes you smile, and *then* makes you do the maths.

The exhibition keeps doing this, in different keys. The [excavator behind the beetle](../11-the-machine-behind/) was the sober version. This is the same sentence, said with a smile.

Wrong turn. Yes. But also: there was no right turn to take.
