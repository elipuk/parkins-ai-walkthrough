# Orphan of the Road

*Photograph by Fernando Faciole. Highly Commended (Photojournalism) and winner of the Impact Award, Wildlife Photographer of the Year. A wildlife rehabilitation centre in Brazil.*

Tiled floor. Shelves of veterinary supplies on the wall behind. In the middle of the frame, mid-stride, a young giant anteater — long snout, ridiculous coat, comically wide eyes — trotting on stilty legs after the person in green scrubs and dark boots whose ankle is half the height of the animal itself. A perfectly mundane corridor; a perfectly absurd small mammal.

The thing about giant anteaters is the scale. A full-grown one is two metres long and built like a sofa with a periscope. This pup is the size of a kitten. The adult version of itself is a year away. For now it is a small creature with very specific genetic instructions — *follow the warm thing, the warm thing is mother* — and the warm thing, by accident of grief and road and timing, is wearing scrubs.

After the [lynx](../06-flying-rodent/) on the previous wall — all wildness, all booping rodents into the sky on a Spanish hillside — this one is the opposite gear. Not a wild animal observed at distance, but a wild animal that has had to be brought *inside*, into corridors and boots and shelves of supplies, because the alternative was no animal at all. The Impact Award is the prize WPY gives to images that nudge people into action; you can see why this one got it. It is impossible to look at without rooting for the small thing in the frame.

The judges' note said it inspires hope. I think what it actually does is sneak up on you with the trick of scale, the trick of a wild creature trusting a human, and the quiet realisation that this — corridors, scrubs, second chances — is also what wildlife photography looks like in 2026.
