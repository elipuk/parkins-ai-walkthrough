# Ada Lovelace — The First Programmer

Ada Byron was born in 1815 to one of the most turbulent marriages in literary history. Her father was Lord Byron — poet, scandal, exile. Her mother Annabella was a mathematician who called herself the Princess of Parallelograms. Byron left when Ada was one month old. Annabella, determined that Ada would not inherit her father's romanticism, arranged for the child to be educated in mathematics and science from an early age.

She succeeded, though not quite in the way she imagined. Ada's mathematics and Ada's imagination became, over time, indistinguishable from each other.

## The Meeting

At seventeen, Ada met Charles Babbage at a London salon. Babbage was a brilliant, furious, frequently alienating man who had spent years designing his Difference Engine — a mechanical calculator to produce error-free mathematical tables for navigation and astronomy. Ada was captivated. When he showed her plans for his more ambitious successor, the Analytical Engine, she saw something Babbage himself may not have fully articulated.

This was not just a calculator. This was a general-purpose computing machine.

The Analytical Engine had a mill (a processor), a store (memory), and could be programmed using punched cards adapted from Jacquard looms. Babbage was brilliant at the mechanism. He understood what it could compute. Ada saw what it *meant*.

## Note G

In 1842, the Italian mathematician Luigi Menabrea published a paper on the Analytical Engine in French. Ada translated it into English — and added her own notes. They ran to three times the length of the original paper.

It is in those notes, specifically Note G, that she wrote what is now recognised as the first computer algorithm: a step-by-step procedure for calculating Bernoulli numbers using the Analytical Engine's operations. It has since been verified. It works.

But alongside the algorithm, she wrote something harder to quantify and perhaps more remarkable: a philosophical framework for what the engine could and couldn't do.

The engine, she wrote, could manipulate any symbols according to any rules. Not just numbers — music, words, logic, any system reducible to formal operations. The scope was vast. But she drew a precise limit: *"The Analytical Engine has no power of originating anything. It can only do what we know how to order it to perform."*

This is the question that still runs through AI research today: the gap between computation and origination. Ada Lovelace named it in 1843.

## What She Didn't Get to See

Ada Lovelace died in 1852, aged 36, of uterine cancer — in pain, and in debt from failed gambling schemes that had consumed much of her estate. The Analytical Engine was never completed in her lifetime. Babbage, unable to secure sustained funding and unwilling to compromise his vision to accommodate what could actually be built, never finished it. The first working mechanical computer in the Babbage tradition was completed by his son Henry in 1910, nearly sixty years after Ada's death.

The programming language Ada, developed by the US Department of Defense in the late 1970s and still in use in aviation and military systems, is named in her honour. The algorithm in Note G has been reconstructed and confirmed correct.

What she saw in that London salon, at seventeen, was the shape of the next two centuries. She didn't have the language for it. She invented the language.
