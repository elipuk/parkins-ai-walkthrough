# Tim Berners-Lee — The Web as Gift

In March 1989, a physicist at CERN named Tim Berners-Lee submitted a proposal to his supervisor Mike Sendall. The proposal was for an information management system — a way to keep track of the hundreds of researchers, experiments, and evolving findings that made up the complex, distributed organism of a particle physics laboratory. CERN was a place where people arrived, left, arrived again, and nobody could find anything anyone else had done.

Sendall scrawled three words on the cover page: *"Vague but exciting."* He didn't require a formal project plan. He just let Berners-Lee keep working.

That document — modest, tentative, written partly because Berners-Lee was frustrated that CERN kept losing its institutional memory — became the founding specification of the World Wide Web.

## The Making

Berners-Lee grew up in London, the son of two mathematicians who had worked on the Ferranti Mark 1, one of the first commercial computers sold in Britain. He studied physics at Oxford, built his first computer from a television set and a soldering iron, and joined CERN in 1980 as a consultant.

In that first stint, he wrote a personal program called Enquire — named after the Victorian reference book *Enquire Within Upon Everything* — a way of connecting documents to each other through arbitrary associations, like a web of index cards. It ran only on his own computer. When he left CERN, the program was lost.

In 1989, back at CERN and working on the problem of distributed information at scale, he reached further. His proposal combined three inventions: URLs (a universal addressing scheme that could locate any document, anywhere), HTTP (a protocol for transferring documents between computers), and HTML (a markup language for creating hyperlinked documents). The first web server ran on a NeXT computer on his desk in Geneva. The first web page described the World Wide Web project itself.

## The Gift

What happened next is the part that remains, decades later, genuinely surprising.

Berners-Lee and CERN could have patented the web. The protocols, the standards, the addressing scheme — all of it could have been licensed. A small royalty on every web transaction would have made CERN fabulously wealthy and Tim Berners-Lee one of the richest people on earth.

In 1993, CERN released the web to the public domain — no patents, no royalties, no permission required, available to everyone, forever. Asked why, Berners-Lee has given variations on the same answer for thirty years: because it only works if everyone can use it. A web that required permission would have fragmented into competing proprietary systems. The gift wasn't charity. It was the architecture. Openness was the engineering.

## What Followed

Berners-Lee has spent much of the subsequent period watching, with increasing alarm, what has been built on the gift. The concentration of power in a small number of platforms, the weaponisation of personal data, the erosion of privacy, the systematic spread of disinformation — these are not accidents of technology, he has argued. They are choices made by people building on a neutral infrastructure that was designed to enable them, not prevent them.

He has responded by proposing a Magna Carta for the web, launching a Contract for the Web initiative, and since 2018 developing Solid — a technical project to decentralise the web by giving individuals sovereign control of their own data, held in personal data pods that applications request access to rather than accumulate.

He received a knighthood in 2004 and the Turing Award — the Nobel Prize of computing — in 2016. He turned down a salary from the World Wide Web Consortium so it couldn't be said he was enriching himself from the standards he controlled. He performs at festivals playing synthesisers in a band.

The web currently has over 1.9 billion websites and connects approximately five billion people. It began with a proposal a manager called vague but exciting, and a physicist who decided not to own what he'd made.
