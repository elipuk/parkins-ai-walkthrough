# Grace Hopper — The Admiral Who Taught Machines English

Grace Murray Hopper was born in New York in 1906, the eldest of three children in a family that believed, practically and fiercely, that education was not a luxury but an obligation. She studied mathematics and physics at Vassar, earned her doctorate from Yale, and was teaching mathematics at Vassar when Japan bombed Pearl Harbor.

She enlisted in the United States Navy Reserve in 1943, aged 37. The recruiting officer rejected her initially — too old, too light, her work as a mathematics professor too important to the war effort. She appealed, passed a second physical, and was commissioned as a lieutenant junior grade. She was assigned to the Bureau of Ordnance Computation Project at Harvard, where she worked on the Mark I, one of the first large-scale electromechanical computers in the United States. She became its third programmer.

## The Compiler

Her most consequential contribution came in the early 1950s, when she was working at Remington Rand on the UNIVAC. She was convinced that computers did not need to be programmed in machine code — in the raw binary instructions that only engineers could write and no one could easily read. A higher-level language, closer to English, could be compiled down to machine instructions automatically.

The received wisdom was that this was impossible. Computers, people said, couldn't understand English. Hopper's response, characteristically, was to build the thing and see.

She built A-0, the first compiler, in 1952. A compiler takes human-readable instructions and translates them into machine code. It worked. She reported the result. It was largely ignored. She built several more compilers through the decade, each time fighting the same institutional scepticism: that machines couldn't be trusted to do what programmers did, that efficiency would be lost in translation.

She was proved right each time.

## COBOL

Her direct contribution to COBOL — Common Business-Oriented Language, released in 1960 — was pivotal. COBOL was designed to be readable by non-mathematicians: business managers, government clerks, the people who actually needed to use computers for payroll, inventory, and administration. The syntax was deliberately close to plain English sentences.

It was mocked at the time by computer scientists who found it inelegant. It is still running today. COBOL processes an estimated $3 trillion in daily financial transactions worldwide. The systems that clear your mortgage payments, reconcile Social Security records, and settle stock trades were, many of them, written in COBOL. Hopper's elegant-enough language has outlasted most of the people who sneered at it.

## The Nanosecond

Hopper was famously impatient with bureaucracy, and famously good at explaining why. Her most repeated aphorism: *"It's easier to ask forgiveness than permission."* She kept a clock on her wall that ran backwards, to remind visitors that the conventional wisdom about what was impossible was often simply conventional.

She distributed nanoseconds — pieces of wire 11.8 inches long, the exact distance light travels in a billionth of a second — to everyone she met. Admirals, senators, young engineers. The nanosecond was a teaching tool: this is how much time you waste when you make a bad architectural decision, when you use a slow algorithm, when you don't think carefully about what you're asking the machine to do.

She retired from the Navy as a Rear Admiral in 1986, aged 79, the oldest active-duty commissioned officer in the United States Armed Forces. When she died in 1992, she was buried with full military honours at Arlington National Cemetery.

The USS Hopper, a guided-missile destroyer, was commissioned in 1997. The actual moth — removed from the Mark II relay at Harvard in 1947, taped into the logbook with the annotation "First actual case of bug being found" — is in the Smithsonian. She didn't invent the term "bug". She just gave it its permanent name.
