# Alan Turing — The Mind That Built the Machine

Alan Turing was born in London in 1912, the son of a civil servant stationed in India. He grew up in a series of English boarding schools, quietly eccentric, more interested in the patterns of daisies and the logic of chess than the rules of cricket. By the time he arrived at King's College Cambridge in 1931, it was clear that his mind worked differently from other minds — not just faster, but in a different direction entirely.

## The Universal Machine

His 1936 paper, *On Computable Numbers*, is one of the most consequential documents in intellectual history. In it, Turing described an abstract machine — now called the Turing Machine — capable of performing any computation that could be described by a finite set of rules. He proved, almost as a side effect, that some problems are fundamentally unsolvable: there are questions a machine cannot answer, not through lack of power, but through mathematical necessity.

But the deeper gift was the idea that computation itself could be mechanised. That thinking — or at least a version of it — could be done by a machine built from tape, symbols, and a set of rules. Every computer ever made is, in essence, a physical implementation of the Turing Machine.

## Bletchley Park

When war came, Turing found himself at Bletchley Park, the country house in Buckinghamshire that Britain had requisitioned for its codebreaking operation. The German Enigma machine produced billions of possible cipher configurations; the Polish mathematicians who had first broken it had been overwhelmed by an upgraded version with more rotors.

Working with mathematician Gordon Welchman, Turing designed the Bombe: an electromechanical device that systematically eliminated impossible Enigma settings by exploiting predictable phrases in German messages — weather reports, standardised greetings, the word "Heil Hitler" in a certain position. By the war's end, British intelligence was reading German naval communications almost in real time.

How many lives the Bombe saved is impossible to calculate precisely, but historians have estimated it shortened the war by two years. Churchill called the codebreakers his secret weapon. Turing was at the centre of it.

## Can Machines Think?

After the war, Turing turned to the question that had always haunted him. His 1950 paper *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* opened with a deceptively simple challenge: "I propose to consider the question, can machines think?"

He reframed it as the Imitation Game — now called the Turing Test — in which a machine that can convince a human interrogator it is human would be judged, provisionally, to think. It was not a definition of thought. It was a test you could run. Practical. Falsifiable. Infuriating.

The question still runs. The test is still contested. The paper, seventy-five years later, is still the most-cited document in artificial intelligence.

## The Cost

In 1952, Turing was prosecuted for gross indecency. Homosexuality was then a criminal offence in Britain. He was offered a choice between imprisonment and chemical castration — oestrogen injections, intended to suppress libido. He chose the injections. He died in June 1954, aged 41, with a half-eaten apple beside him. The coroner ruled suicide.

In 2013, Queen Elizabeth II granted him a posthumous royal pardon. It was a gesture — sincere and insufficient in equal measure. His face is now on the British fifty-pound note.

The Turing Machine was never built. It didn't need to be. It is a thought experiment with the weight of physical reality — the conceptual foundation on which everything that followed was built.
